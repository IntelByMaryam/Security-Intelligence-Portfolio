# Breif 001: Security Implications of Military Governments in the Sahel
## Executive Summary
## Background
### MALI

The situation in Mali deteriorated significantly amid widespread protests following the
legislative elections of 29 March and 19 April 2020, culminating in a coup d'état on
18 August 2020. The protests were triggered by the decision of the Constitutional Court
on 30 April to overturn the preliminary results of the legislative elections for 31 of
the 147 seats, mostly in favour of candidates of the ruling coalition.

On the morning of 18 August, a group of soldiers and army officers led a mutiny where the President Ibrahim Boubacar Keïta, 
the Prime Minister, and other high-ranking government and military officials were detained. 
Around midnight, in a broadcast on national television, the President announced his resignation 
and the dissolution of the government and National Assembly. Early on 19 August, the officers
announced they had designated themselves the National Committee for the Safeguard of the
People "Comité National pour le Salut du Peuple" (CNSP) with Colonel Assimi Goïta,
commander of a special forces battalion based in central Mali, as President. The group
stated its actions were motivated by the need to fight corruption, bad governance, and to
end instability in central and northern Mali.

In November 2020, in a bid to present a civilian transition to the international
community, a transitional president, Bah N'Daw, and Prime Minister, Moctar Ouane, were
installed. Nine months later, on 24 May 2021, military officers arrested N'Daw and Ouane
on the orders of Assimi Goïta, who was serving as Vice President and head of the Junta.
N'Daw and Ouane's cabinet had removed two former Junta members from the Ministries of
Defence and Security, replacing them with officers unaffiliated with Goïta, without his
approval, a direct challenge to Junta authority that Goïta moved swiftly to reverse.

### BURKINA FASO

Burkina Faso experienced two coups within nine months. The first, on 24 January 2022,
was led by Lieutenant Colonel Paul-Henri Sandaogo Damiba, who cited the government's
failure to contain the jihadist insurgency as justification for the takeover. Before a
transitional government could be consolidated, Captain Ibrahim Traoré, head of an
artillery unit of the Armed Forces, declared himself head of state on 30 September 2022,
citing the same grounds, Burkina Faso's deteriorating security situation. Traoré's
Junta hastily organised a two-day national forum on 14 and 15 October, with Junta
representatives dominating the proceedings, at which he was designated transitional
president. The junta retained significant authority under the transitional governing
charter.

### NIGER

On 26 July 2023, soldiers loyal to the Presidential Guard detained democratically elected
President Mohamed Bazoum. Two days later, the head of the Presidential Guard,
Abdourahamane Tchiani, named himself head of the Nigerien transitional government and
head of the National Council for the Safeguard of the Homeland "Conseil National pour
la Sauvegarde de la Patrie" (CNSP). Tchiani justified his actions as necessary to avoid
the inevitable demise of the country, accusing Bazoum of projecting a false sense of
security while the "harsh reality is a pile of dead, displaced, humiliation and
frustration."


Each Junta leader attributed their seizure of power to insecurity, bad governance, and
the failure of civilian governments to mount an effective response to the jihadist threat.
Armed groups linked to the Islamic State (IS) and al-Qaeda have operated across the
tri-border area shared by Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger for over a decade, driving a
long-running conflict that successive civilian governments failed to contain. This shared
grievance, state failure in the face of persistent jihadist violence gave each Junta
a degree of popular legitimacy that complicated the international community's response
and reduced the effectiveness of external pressure to restore civilian rule.

The coups drew immediate condemnation from regional and international bodies. ECOWAS
suspended all three countries from its institutions following each takeover and imposed
graduated sanctions, including travel bans, asset freezes, and trade restrictions
while demanding returns to civilian constitutional order.

The most significant and consequential response came after the Niger coup in July 2023.
Under the chairmanship of Nigerian President Bola Tinubu, ECOWAS imposed comprehensive
economic sanctions on Niger, threatened to close member state borders, and issued a
deadline warning of potential military intervention to restore President Bazoum to power.
This threat proved to be a strategic miscalculation. Rather than isolating Niger's Junta,
it galvanised solidarity among all three military governments and accelerated their
collective break from the regional bloc. Mali and Burkina Faso declared that any military
action against Niger would be treated as an act of war against them as well.

At the broader international level, the African Union called for returns to constitutional
governance but lacked the enforcement mechanisms to compel compliance. France, whose
military presence in the Sahel had expanded through Operation Serval (2013) and its
successor, Operation Barkhane was expelled from Mali in January 2023 and from Burkina
Faso shortly after, following years of mounting anti-French public sentiment and protests.
Niger expelled French forces in 2024. The United States, which operated significant
counterterrorism infrastructure in Niger, including the $110 million drone surveillance
facility in Agadez was similarly ordered to withdraw by 2024, eliminating Washington's
most capable surveillance and strike asset in the central Sahel. The United Nations
peacekeeping mission in Mali, MINUSMA, which had operated since 2013, was forced to
withdraw by the end of 2023 after the Malian transitional government demanded its
departure.

It is important to note that the isolation of the three countries was not solely a
product of ECOWAS sanctions. The juntas also made deliberate, independent choices by
rejecting civilian transition timelines, terminating Western defence agreements, and
actively seeking alternative security partnerships, that deepened their separation from
established regional and international frameworks.

### FORMATION OF THE AES

Against the backdrop of ECOWAS's military intervention threat against Niger, and driven
by a shared interest in collective defence, Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger established
the Alliance of Sahel States (AES) on 16 September 2023. The founding charter committed
the three states to collective defence and mutual assistance, uniting their efforts
against external threats.

The AES subsequently evolved from a defence pact into a full confederation, formalised
by treaty in July 2024. On 28 January 2025, all three countries formally withdrew from
ECOWAS, a departure the bloc acknowledged as a significant rupture in West African
regional integration, marking the end of a decade-long diplomatic effort to hold the
Sahel within the framework of collective regional governance.
